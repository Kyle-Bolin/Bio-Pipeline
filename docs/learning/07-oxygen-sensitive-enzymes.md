# 07 · Oxygen-sensitive enzymes: a reaction the model runs and the cell can't

Every check so far asked whether a route is possible on paper: balanced, downhill, forced by deletions, affordable in protein. This one asks a blunter question — could this particular enzyme even exist in the conditions we just simulated?

**Oxygen is chemically aggressive.** O₂ is a diradical: it has two unpaired electrons, and it reacts readily with anything else carrying an unpaired electron or a low-potential metal centre. A whole family of enzymes relies on exactly those features. *Pyruvate formate-lyase* (PFL, gene *pflB*) carries a radical on one of its own glycine residues and uses it to split pyruvate into acetyl-CoA and formate. Oxygen attacks that radical and the enzyme does not recover; reports describe the polypeptide being cleaved at the radical site. On top of that, the enzyme has to be switched on by a separate activating protein that is itself oxygen-sensitive, and *E. coli* mostly transcribes *pflB* when oxygen is absent. So the reaction is unavailable in air twice over: the cell barely makes the enzyme, and what it makes is destroyed.

The same logic covers the other entries in this module's table: the pyruvate:flavodoxin/ferredoxin oxidoreductases, the Ni-Fe and Fe-Fe hydrogenases, nitrogenase with its iron-molybdenum cofactor, and the **class III ribonucleotide reductase**, another glycyl radical enzyme. *E. coli* keeps a whole second ribonucleotide reductase, class I, for aerobic use — which is a nice illustration that cells solve this problem by having two enzymes for one job, not by making one enzyme tolerate everything.

**Fragile is not the same as anaerobe-only, and this is where it is easy to overreach.** Two counterexamples kept this module's table honest. The B12-dependent diol and glycerol dehydratases make a radical from their cobalamin cofactor on every turnover and do get inactivated, partly by oxygen — but the cell carries a *reactivase* that swaps in a fresh cofactor, and aerobic *E. coli* making 1,3-propanediol with *Klebsiella* dehydratase plus that reactivase is a commercial process. They are left out of the table. And *E. coli*'s hydrogenase-1 is the textbook **oxygen-tolerant** Ni-Fe hydrogenase: it keeps oxidising H₂ in air. It stays in the table, because its genes are still expressed mainly anaerobically, but the warning for it means "the cell probably isn't making much of this", not "oxygen destroys it". Since a flag here can declare a strain design unbuildable, the difference between "destroyed", "inhibited and recovers", "repaired by the cell" and "only ever expressed without oxygen" is the whole game.

**Jargon.** A *radical* is a molecule with an unpaired electron, reactive and short-lived. A *glycyl radical enzyme* parks one on a glycine in its own backbone. *Oxygen-sensitive* here means the protein itself is damaged, not that oxygen competes for the active site. A *reactivase* is a helper protein that repairs a spent cofactor so the enzyme can turn over again.

**Why the model doesn't know.** A genome-scale model is stoichiometry plus flux bounds. "This protein falls apart in air" is a fact about protein chemistry and gene regulation, and there is nowhere in a stoichiometric matrix to put it. BiGG models ship one set of bounds for all conditions, so PFL sits there with an upper bound of 1000 whether or not the medium contains oxygen. FBA will use it when it is convenient. In PR #17 that was not hypothetical: the best OptKnock design for aerobic succinate deleted pyruvate dehydrogenase and pushed acetyl-CoA through PFL instead — a strain that cannot work, with an attractive predicted yield.

**What this module reports, and what it doesn't.** It matches suspect reactions by EC number and enzyme name, checks whether they carry flux in a flux state that actually consumes oxygen, and then does one extra thing: it closes each flagged reaction and re-solves. If the answer doesn't change, the solver merely picked an anaerobic enzyme out of several equally good routes — the number stands, the route shown is wrong. If the answer drops, the prediction genuinely rests on an enzyme oxygen destroys. In `iJO1366` the first case shows up immediately: parsimonious FBA routes deoxyribonucleotide synthesis through the *anaerobic* class III reductase in full air, probably because that route is one reaction shorter, even though the aerobic class I enzyme is in the model. Nothing is closed on your behalf: the warning is a warning.

**The nuance the check flattens.** It treats a flux state as aerobic or not, and real cultures are not binary. *E. coli* runs mixed-acid fermentation at low dissolved oxygen, and a stirred tank has microaerobic pockets, so some PFL flux in a nominally aerated vessel is not impossible — it is impossible in the fully aerobic steady state the model solved for. I'd rather say that plainly than pretend the threshold is sharp.

**What a biologist would ask that this code cannot answer.** At what dissolved oxygen concentration does this enzyme actually retain activity, and was that measured in a cell or on purified protein? How much flux could the small amount of surviving enzyme carry? Does an engineered or naturally oxygen-tolerant variant exist, or a repair system the host could express? And for the class III reductase result: is the model's choice of route just a tie in the solver, or does the cell really use it a little in air? None of those are in the stoichiometry, which is why this module's output is a flag for a human to judge, not a verdict.
