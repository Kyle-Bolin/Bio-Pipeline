Flux balance analysis · genome-scale models

Which microbe should make your vanillin? And your lycopene?

I gave two workhorse microbes, E. coli and baker's yeast S. cerevisiae, the genes to make two compounds neither makes naturally, then asked their genome-scale metabolic models how much product a gram of glucose could become. The answer flips between the two molecules, and the model says why.

Ferrous (Kyle Bolin) · · Kyle-Bolin/Bio-Pipeline

The short answer

Theoretical maximum yield: grams of product per gram of glucose if the cell put every spare carbon into product instead of growing. Real strains reach a fraction of this, but the ceiling decides which host is worth engineering.

What I added to each cell

Neither organism has these enzymes, so each model gets three new reactions borrowed from other species, attached to a precursor the cell already makes. Every added reaction is checked for balanced atoms and charge.

Vanillin C₈H₈O₃ · vanilla flavour

  1. glucose → shikimate pathway
  2. 3-dehydroshikimate
  3. aroZ dehydratase · fungus Podospora
  4. protocatechuic acid
  5. car carboxylic acid reductase · Nocardia · ATP, NADPH
  6. protocatechualdehyde
  7. hsOMT O-methyltransferase · human · SAM
  8. vanillin

Route from Hansen et al. 2009, who built it in yeast.

Lycopene C₄₀H₅₆ · the red in tomatoes

  1. glucose → isoprenoid pathway (MEP in E. coli, mevalonate in yeast)
  2. IPP + FPP (5- and 15-carbon building blocks)
  3. crtE GGPP synthase · Pantoea (yeast already has one, BTS1)
  4. geranylgeranyl diphosphate (C₂₀)
  5. crtB phytoene synthase · joins two C₂₀ units
  6. phytoene (C₄₀)
  7. crtI phytoene desaturase · 4 desaturations, O₂
  8. lycopene

The crtEBI genes, first expressed in E. coli by Misawa et al. 1990.

Ceiling yields, three ways

Mass yield is what an industrial buyer cares about. Carbon yield shows how much of the sugar's carbon lands in product; the rest leaves as CO₂ to pay for energy and electrons.

Why the winner flips

Lycopene: E. coli's isoprenoid route wastes less carbon

Lycopene is built from eight 5-carbon IPP units. E. coli makes IPP by the MEP pathway, starting from pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Yeast uses the mevalonate pathway, which builds IPP from three acetyl-CoA (six carbons) and throws one away as CO₂, after already losing a CO₂ for every acetyl-CoA made from pyruvate.

Vanillin: E. coli throws away a sugar every time it methylates

Both cells can make the precursor equally well (). The difference is the last step. Adding a methyl group uses SAM and leaves S-adenosyl-homocysteine (SAH). Yeast splits SAH with SAH hydrolase (SAH1) and recycles the adenosine. E. coli has no SAH hydrolase: it uses Pfs and LuxS, which release the ribose as the signalling molecule AI-2, five carbons lost per vanillin.

The growth tax

A cell that grows is spending glucose on itself. These production envelopes show the most product each cell could make at every growth rate. At its fastest growth, neither organism is forced to make any product: these compounds are not coupled to growth, so an unengineered strain has no reason to make them.

Which genes to delete

Deleting a gene can push carbon toward the product. A standard flux balance search finds nothing here, because none of these products is coupled to growth. So the tool uses MOMA (minimisation of metabolic adjustment): it starts from a strain making a little product and predicts how a fresh mutant reroutes its fluxes while staying as close as possible to its parent. It then greedily adds the deletion that most raises product per glucose, keeping growth above 30% of the parent's.

Sanity checks

A model prediction is only as good as the model. Before trusting the numbers above, each model has to pass checks where the answer is already known.

What this doesn't tell you

  • These are stoichiometric ceilings. Real titers are limited by enzyme speed, gene expression, and toxicity, none of which flux balance analysis models. Vanillin is toxic to both hosts, and yeast's ADH6 reduces it to vanillyl alcohol.
  • Both models assume unlimited oxygen. Yeast's real preference for fermenting glucose to ethanol even with oxygen (the Crabtree effect) is not captured.
  • The car enzyme needs activation by a phosphopantetheinyl transferase, and lycopene accumulates in membranes; neither cost is modelled.
  • MOMA predictions are hypotheses for a first round of strain building, not guarantees. The SAH1 idea is a model suggestion I have not seen tested.
  • E. coli uses iJO1366 (2011); the newer iML1515 was not reachable from my build environment. Rerun with --model iML1515 to compare.

Reproduce it

Every number and chart on this page comes from one script in the repository. It downloads the pinned yeast model on first run and takes about 30 minutes with the knockout search.

git clone https://github.com/Kyle-Bolin/Bio-Pipeline && cd Bio-Pipeline
pip install -e .
python scripts/showcase.py              # writes docs/showcase/results.json + charts
python scripts/showcase.py --knockouts 0  # yields only, about a minute
